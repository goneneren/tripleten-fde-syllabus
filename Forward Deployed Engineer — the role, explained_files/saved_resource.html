Forward Deployed Engineer: what the job actually is

Written for marketing, sales and product. Three questions: what this profession really is, what a course for it has to contain, and how to sell it to an audience that assumes it is being lied to.

Part 1

What the profession is

The work itself, the titles around it, who hires it, and what the postings demand.

The definition the market agrees on

The title is contested, the work isn't. Across 53 postings, the same job description keeps appearing under five different names.

An engineer who goes into someone else's business, works out what should be built there, and builds it — in the client's environment, with the client in the room.

Palantir invented the title over a decade ago. What's new isn't the role, it's the cargo: the thing being deployed is now an AI system that behaves differently in every customer's mess. That's why hiring exploded, and why the same work is posted as Applied AI Engineer, Forward Deployed AI Engineer, FDSE, AI Applications Engineer, or Founding FDE.

How the work runs, start to finish

01DiscoverySit with the client's team and trace how the work really happens — not how the process document says it happens.
02ScopeDecide what's worth building, what to cut, and where AI has no business being. Estimate it and defend the estimate.
03BuildWrite production integration code against undocumented client systems. Ship an agent that survives the unhappy path.
04DeployGet it live inside the client's cloud, security review, permissions and all. Prove it works with evals, not with a demo.
05Hand backDrive adoption, then turn what you learned into a reusable playbook so the next deployment is cheaper.

What it does, and what it does not

The job does
  • Write production code that ships into a customer's stack — not slides, not diagrams
  • Own delivery end to end, from the first conversation to the system running in production
  • Decide what gets built, including saying that a process should be fixed with plain software and no AI
  • Carry the technical relationship with people from engineers to the CFO
  • Feed the field back into product — patterns, playbooks, feature requests
The job does not
  • Train or fine-tune models. Zero of 41 roles ask for it. That's a different profession
  • Carry a sales quota — in the real version. Where it does, that's the disguised sales role candidates fear
  • Manage people. Growth goes to designing bigger systems, not bigger teams
  • Own the product roadmap. An FDE optimises for one customer; coherence across customers belongs to someone else
  • Live in one codebase. No greenfield, no months of deep focus on a single problem

Same job, other names

Forward Deployed AI Engineer · FDSE · Founding FDE · FD Infrastructure Engineer. And Applied AI Engineer at the frontier labs only — elsewhere that title means the same work minus the customer.

Source candidates here

Implementation, onboarding and professional-services engineers hold the client half. Senior backend, platform and data engineers hold the engineering half. Solutions architects and SREs are two steps away.

Delete from every search

“Integration engineer” returns hardware roles. “Field engineer” returns department heads — 27 of 32 hits are Director or Manager of Field Engineering. And “Reliability” inside the title is a different, cheaper job.

What the postings actually require

Read line by line across 41 unique roles. Two findings surprise people: the degree barely matters, and the common core is smaller than you'd expect.

Education

A diploma is not the gate. One posting in 41 makes it a hard requirement.

  • Roughly half mention a degree at all — nearly always as “or equivalent” or preferred
  • Master's or PhD appears as preferred exactly once, at Google
  • Named fields are broad: CS, maths, software engineering, physics, data science
  • One posting accepts competitive-programming pedigree instead of experience; another counts client experience from student clubs
Experience

Four years is the median — and proof you shipped beats the number.

  • Years stated in 29 of 41 roles; half of those ask for five or more
  • Twelve roles state no years at all, and say “production-grade”, “shipped at scale”, “not demos” instead
  • Pay tracks the employer tier, not the years behind you
  • Two thirds want prior customer-facing work as evidence — the hardest line on the list to satisfy

Common core versus the specific gate

In almost every posting

Describe these six and you have described the job.

  • Production engineering, one main language, usually Python
  • APIs and integration into enterprise systems
  • Some LLM or GenAI experience — nine in ten
  • Translating between technical and business audiences
  • Owning delivery end to end, with no spec to start from
  • Holding stakeholders, up to executives
Specific — and where it lives

These can't be crammed. They decide which door is open at all.

  • Epic, HL7, FHIR healthcare
  • SIEM, XDR, SOAR, SOC operations security
  • SIP, WebRTC, contact centres voice AI — biggest vertical cluster
  • Graph databases, anti-money-laundering financial crime
  • RL environments, Gym, SWE-bench AI labs
  • Spark runtime internals data platform
  • PLM, MBSE, hardware programmes hardware
  • Apex, Salesforce certifications vendor ladder
  • Go or Rust infra edge — no Python at all
  • US citizenship, clearance to TS/SCI closed to most

The rule this hands a candidate: the common core gets you considered anywhere, the specific requirement decides where you can apply at all. So target the vertical you already have — and teach domain discovery, never the domains.

What has to be there on day one, what we teach, and what a graduate can hand a hiring manager.

What has to be there already, what we teach, what nobody can hand over

The honest split. It also explains the admission bar: we can't sell this to someone without the first lane.

Must arrive with it The engineering gate
  • Years of production engineering — “shipped at scale”, not demos
  • Python, or a language they can carry over
  • Comfort in one cloud, APIs, debugging across layers
  • Median bar across the market: four years, with half the postings that state a number asking five or more

Verified at intake. Teaching this would make us a different school for a different buyer.

We teach it The AI delivery stack and the field method
  • Agents that survive contact with reality: tool use, orchestration, guardrails
  • Retrieval over a client's messy documents
  • Evals and golden datasets — turning “it seems to work” into evidence
  • Observability and debugging of non-deterministic behaviour
  • Discovery, workflow tracing, scoping and estimation
  • ROI in the language of revenue, risk and cost
  • Defending the build to a non-engineer
  • Turning one customer's solution into a reusable playbook
Cannot be handed over A history of client work
  • Two thirds of postings ask for prior customer-facing experience as proof
  • No course can retroactively put a client in someone's past

So we do two things instead: surface what's already there — in the CVs of working FDEs the field muscle came from consulting, private equity, a seat in VC, teaching, running a camp — and manufacture auditable evidence: a client component in every project, public reference architectures, a demo day.

What a graduate can actually point to

Not modules. Five things they can hand a hiring manager, and four things they've done enough times to do them under pressure. Together these stand in for the client history nobody can give them.

Five artefacts — auditable, public
  • A traced workflow. A real, messy process mapped end to end, including the exceptions the process document never mentions
  • An agent with an audit trail. Running on a genuine business task, where you can show exactly how it reached its conclusion
  • Unhappy-path hardening. Schema validation and failure recovery — the part that keeps working when the input is garbage
  • An eval suite. Golden dataset, quality metrics, a before-and-after that proves behaviour instead of asserting it
  • An integration into legacy. Against an undocumented system with real permissions and limits, not a clean sandbox
Four rehearsals — done until they hold
  • A discovery sprint with a live stakeholder who withholds information, changes their mind, and controls a budget
  • A scoping defence: estimate it, cut scope out loud, and hold the line on what isn't included
  • The business defence. One build, two rooms: an architecture review with engineers and a value case for a VP
  • The live interview drill — narrating a solution while the constraints keep changing. The exact format candidates report failing

Why this split matters commercially: the artefacts are what a sceptical audience can check — and this audience assumes course claims are bought. Five public, working things beat any testimonial we could print.

Part 3

How to sell it

What actually hurts, what pays, the objection we can answer with facts, and where the roles are.

What actually hurts, in their words

Ranked by how many of 14 threads raise it independently. Each line is a real pain with a real quote — this is the objection list any campaign has to survive.

  1. 8/14The title itself is suspect before the job is read “I always thought the title was kind of cringey — like someone doing military cosplay, or those coding ninja titles from 10 years ago.”
  2. 8/14Nobody can say how it differs from a solutions architect “Simply sounds like a technical account manager or solutions architect to me.”
  3. 7/14Fear of being handed several jobs and one salary “It's a way companies can package up three roles into one… because they need to operate as cheap as possible.”

What earns their attention
  • Naming the objection before they do — the sales trap, the travel, the solo accountability
  • Technical specificity: schema validation, failure recovery, audit trails, evals
  • Verifiable specifics — instructors who did the job, outcomes that can be checked
What loses them instantly
  • Salary as a promise — that's the competitors' weak flank, not our pitch
  • “A brand-new profession”. The model is over a decade old and they know it
  • Scarcity, guarantees, testimonial volume with no names — they assume reviews are bought
  • Leading with the acronym: no search demand, and the word itself triggers suspicion

Then four things move you inside the band

+$50–100KThe employer tier, not your years. An AI-first company pays that much more than a baseline employer for the identical title.
+ top of bandA named client-delivery track record. The field half is precisely what the upper half of every band is paying for.
−15–25%If the band is labelled OTE, that share is variable and depends on selling. One posting states an 80/20 split outright.
−$60KThe reliability variant of the same title — support rather than discovery and build. Same words, different job.

Equity sits on top and is not in these bars. It runs around 25–30% of total pay at the typical level, and considerably more at the frontier labs — which is where the viral $500K+ screenshots come from. That is paper value at private-company marks, and the same threads that post it argue it down. For anything public we quote one figure instead: $125–198K typical and up to $244K for top earners, Glassdoor total pay across 651 reports. It's a different metric from the bars above — total pay, not base — so never put the two on the same slide.

A ready-made lead magnet: “is this FDE job actually a sales job?”

Not trivia for candidates — this is content we can publish tomorrow. Five of fourteen threads ask for exactly this checklist, the market's own estimate is that a chunk of postings are disguised sales roles, and nobody has published the test. Giving it away answers the audience's loudest objection with facts instead of reassurance, and proves we know the market — which is the credibility bar with people who assume every course buys its reviews.

Why marketing needs this
  • It's the objection killer. “It's secretly a sales job” is the #3 pain in the corpus. You can't argue it away — you hand them the test
  • It's free-tier content with a real job to do: a checklist, a post, a filter in the funnel. Useful before anyone buys anything
  • It disciplines our promise. We can't say “you'll land an FDE role” when the label covers four different jobs. We can say you'll be able to tell them apart
And it fixes our own targeting

The same signals decide which postings we point graduates at, which employers are worth a hiring partnership, and which “FDE” roles we should quietly exclude from any outcomes claim we ever publish. A placement into a quota-carrying sales role is not a placement into this profession.

And who actually got hired

Six forward deployed engineers on one team, all hired inside thirteen months. Profiles described in aggregate — no names, this is personal data.

Worth knowing about the employer first: its three open FDE roles — 3+ years on customer delivery, 6+ years on partner products, and a director role that explicitly rules out sales engineering and professional services — are all measured on reusable outcomes rather than hours delivered. Two honest caveats. This is one early-stage team of six, and it hires cheaper and closer to home than the wider market: median experience at entry is around two years, against a market median of four. So it doesn't lower our admission bar — the product is sold into the market, not into this team. What it does tell us is where the field muscle really comes from. Not one of these people held a client-facing title. It came from consulting, from a fund, from teaching, from running a camp. Our buyer almost certainly has the same thing buried somewhere, and the job is to surface it and name it.
